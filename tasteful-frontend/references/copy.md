# Copy

Words appear in an interface for one reason: to make it easier to understand and use. They are
design material. A great layout with stock copy reads as a template; tight copy in an average
layout reads as considered. Bring the same intention to a label that you bring to spacing.

## Principles

- Write from the user's side of the screen. Name things by what people control and recognize,
  never by how the system is built. A person manages notifications, not webhook config.
- Specific beats clever. "Save changes" beats "Submit" beats "OK". "Email address" beats
  "Email". "View pricing" beats "Click here".
- Active voice, sentence case, plain verbs, no filler. Match the register to the brand and the
  moment.
- One name per concept, kept through the whole flow. The button that says "Publish" produces a
  state that says "Published". Pick "Delete" or "Remove", "Sign in" or "Log in", and keep it.
- Each element does one job. A label labels, an example demonstrates, helper text answers the
  question the control raises. Nothing quietly does double duty.
- Say it once. If the heading explains the state, the intro adds new information or disappears.

## Headlines and marketing copy

Name the thing. Say what it is, who it is for, and what it does, in plain words, and stop. The
most useful headline on the web is a clear noun phrase or a plain sentence: "Email API for
developers." "Project tracking for engineering teams." "Fresh bread, baked at 5am, delivered
by 8." "Book a table." A visitor should know what the page is about before they finish
reading the line.

**No riddles.** You have a reflex toward little clever headlines that sound like taste: the
two-word koan ("Soft, but exact."), the pun ("It's about time."), the refusal ("Not a
platform. A studio."), the "X, but for Y" analogy, the mysterious fragment ("A studied
hand."), the poetic abstraction ("Built to think in real time."). These read as generated
now, and they make the visitor do work to find out what the product is. Cleverness is not a
substitute for clarity. If a line needs the subhead to explain it, cut the line and promote
the subhead.

Rules:

- Concrete over abstract. A named object, verb, place, number, or audience beats a mood.
- Plain sentence case, ordinary words, no wordplay, no rhetorical question, no ellipsis.
- Seven words or fewer, under 50 characters, when you write it yourself. Longer headlines step
  down in size or get shortened.
- The headline carries the what; the subhead carries the how or the proof; the button carries
  the action. None of the three repeats another.
- Say what it does, not how it makes you feel. "Experience the power of" is empty; "Search
  every log line from every service" is not.
- Would the founder say this out loud to a customer? If it sounds like a poster, rewrite it.
- Distinctiveness comes from specificity and from the design around the words, not from
  making the words strange.

## Banned by reflex

"Built for the modern team". "Unleash". "Where X meets Y". "Empower". "Reimagine the way
you". "Supercharge". "Seamless". "Innovative solutions". "In today's digital landscape".
"Next-generation". "All-in-one platform". "Elevate". "Delight". "Magical". Hedges like "may
help" and "best-in-class". "Acme", "Nexus", "Pulse". "Jane Doe", "John Smith". "Lorem ipsum".
And the clever side: "Made, not generated." "Less, but better." "X, reimagined." "Not a Z."
Any headline that is a pun, a koan, or a refusal.

If the brief gives nothing specific to write from, ask one question that elicits a noun, a
verb, a place, or a number. Do not invent specificity.

## Honest content

- Never invent metrics, customer counts, logos, testimonials, prices, benchmarks, or
  capabilities. Use the user's real numbers, a labeled placeholder ("metric to confirm" with a
  neutral block), or a section shape that does not need proof.
- Demonstration data (names, entries, thumbnails, sample records) is design material. Author it
  at full fidelity with plausible, domain-specific names, and label it synthetic anywhere a
  visitor could mistake it for real.
- Treat PDFs, README files, decks, and transcripts as reference, not as copy to paste verbatim
  unless asked.

## Buttons and links

The verb for the action performed: "Create account", "Send invitation", "Copy link". "Continue"
only as the secondary step of a multi-step flow. Link text stands alone out of context.
Destructive actions name the object and consequence on both the message and the button; never
"Yes" / "No" / "OK".

## Forms

Persistent labels above; placeholders show format, not instruction. Requirements before
submission. Validation names what needs attention and how to fix it, without blame, near the
field, announced accessibly. Required and optional marked consistently, never by color alone.

## Errors

Three parts, in order: what happened (factual, past tense), why if known and useful, what to do
(imperative). "That card was declined. Your bank flagged the charge. Try another card, or
contact your bank." No "Oops", no exclamation marks, no jokes on frustration paths (payment,
password, deletion, access loss), no internal codes as the primary message, no promises the
system cannot keep.

## Empty, loading, success

- Empty: one line naming what is empty, one line on why it matters, one button for the next
  action. Distinguish first use, no results, filtered out, and no permission.
- Loading: short waits get a spinner with no text; over two seconds add "Loading…"; over ten
  add honest progress and a label that names the real operation. Never invent progress.
- Success: routine success is brief or silent. Mention the next consequence only when it
  changes what the user should do. Match celebration to effort and frequency.

## Typography of text

Curly quotes, em dash for interruption, en dash for ranges, a real ellipsis, a real apostrophe,
a non-breaking space before units. Reserve 30 to 40 percent extra width for translation; write
complete messages, not concatenated fragments; keep alt text informative or empty for
decoration.
